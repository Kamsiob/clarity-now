# Clarity Now: Design and Structure Review

A four round expert panel, convened to audit the app before any code is written.

**Nothing in this document has been implemented.** It is a recommendation set awaiting approval. Each recommendation carries a number, a rationale, a cost, and a confidence level.

**A note on the panel.** The six panelists below are composite roles, not real people. I am playing each of them against a distinct evidence base. The research each cites is real and sourced; the personalities are a device for forcing genuine disagreement rather than a single voice agreeing with itself. The focus group personas are likewise composites built from documented user behaviour patterns.

---

## The panel

**Ines R.** Behavioural scientist. Personal informatics, self-regulation, intention-behaviour gap.
**Marcus D.** Design director. Visual identity systems, brand differentiation.
**Priya N.** UX researcher. Mobile interaction, information architecture, density.
**Tomas L.** Editorial designer. Long form typography, information design, pacing.
**Dr. Hanne V.** Clinical psychologist. Motivation, self-determination theory, digital wellbeing.
**Ray O.** Indie app operator. Shipped and maintained solo productivity apps.

**Focus group.** Six composite users, introduced in round four.

# ROUND ONE: INDEPENDENT FINDINGS

Each panelist researched their own domain before seeing the others' work.

## Marcus D., design director

I ran the design system against the current literature on machine-generated design signatures. The findings are worse than I expected and they are not a matter of taste.

<cite index="53-1">A study of 1,590 Show HN landing pages scored each against sixteen DOM and CSS patterns that designers identified as tells. The list includes: Inter font everywhere, repeated font combos like Space Grotesk and Instrument Serif, serif italics for accent words, dark mode with low-contrast body text, colored left borders on cards, all-caps section labels.</cite> <cite index="53-1">More than half of what is currently launching carries a visual fingerprint that reads as generated by a chat interface without an opinion.</cite>

Clarity Now currently lands **six** of those sixteen.

1. **The coloured left border.** Multiple independent sources name this specifically. <cite index="46-1">A colored 3 to 4px left-border strip is described as the single most reliable AI tell.</cite> <cite index="52-1">A thick colored border on one side of a rounded card is called the single most recognizable tell of AI-generated UI, and the guidance is to use a subtler accent or remove it entirely.</cite> <cite index="49-1">One anti-slop reference calls the rounded card with a colored left-border accent the canonical AI dashboard tile shape and instructs dropping either the radius or the border.</cite> Karim caught this unprompted, which tells you how legible it is.

2. **Instrument Serif with Inter.** This is the painful one, because it is inherited from the shipped iOS app. <cite index="62-1">Font tooling now describes this pairing as the trending editorial combination, currently very popular in design, and ships ready-made prompts instructing AI tools to use exactly it.</cite> That is the mechanism by which a good 2024 choice becomes a 2026 fingerprint. <cite index="50-1">Inter is named as part of the core AI slop fingerprint, alongside the indigo gradient and the row of three rounded cards.</cite>

3. **All-caps section labels.** On the tell list. `YOUR WEEK, HONESTLY` and every `sectionCaps` label in the system.

4. **Serif italics for accent words.** On the tell list. The `ONE THING THIS WEEK` line.

5. **Hairline border plus diffuse shadow on the same element.** <cite index="52-1">A hairline border paired with a wide, diffuse shadow is described as a recurring generated-UI signature, with the instruction to commit to one: a defined edge or a soft elevation, not both.</cite> Our chips, cards and settings rows do both.

6. **Cardocalypse.** <cite index="46-1">Cards nested inside cards is named directly, and a grey box around every card is called the number one slop tell. The prescribed order is whitespace first, then a 3 to 5 percent background lightness shift, then soft elevation, and a border only if all three fail.</cite> The settings screen has rows inside groups inside a sheet. The area detail sheet has a card inside a sheet.

The root cause is well understood. <cite index="47-1">The model reverts to the centre whenever a step is left open, and with an under-specified prompt the safest single output is the average, which is the slop.</cite> Our design.md is highly specified, which is why we avoided the purple gradient and the three-card row. But specification inherited from a document written before these tells crystallised does not protect you.

## Ines R., behavioural scientist

I looked at whether the Report can do what it claims. The literature is not encouraging about apps like this, and that is useful.

<cite index="69-1">A review of self-tracking found that insights gained through reflection on personal data are frequently not actionable towards behaviour change. Many systems tacitly assume that reflection will occur naturally once data is processed and visualised, while most theories of reflection highlight that reflection needs to be explicitly supported.</cite>

That is our exact failure mode. We have built an extremely sophisticated observation engine and then assumed that reading a beautiful paragraph produces reflection. It does not.

<cite index="68-1">Work on reflective informatics argues that many tools conflate reflection with compliance, offering limited support for interpretation, curiosity, or sensemaking beyond numerical feedback.</cite> We are better than most here, because our output is prose rather than a dashboard. But we still stop at presentation.

The strongest available intervention is not more observation. It is a change in the form of the closing line.

<cite index="72-1">Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis of 94 independent studies found implementation intentions produced an effect size of d equals 0.65 on goal achievement, meaning the average person using if-then plans outperformed roughly 74 percent of people who set goals without a specific plan.</cite> <cite index="72-1">In the original 1997 study, participants forming implementation intentions completed the task at 71 percent against 32 percent for those with goal intentions only.</cite>

The mechanism is precise. <cite index="74-1">A goal intention specifies an outcome. An implementation intention specifies the when, where and how of the behaviour that will produce it. The first tells you the destination.</cite>

Our `ONE THING THIS WEEK` is currently a goal intention. *Before adding anything new, try finishing one thing already in a queue.* That is the weak form, and it is the form the meta-analysis measured against.

The strong form requires a cue. And here is what makes this interesting for us specifically: **we already know the user's cues.** We have their busiest day, their most productive time of day, the day their focus sessions actually happen. No other app in this category has that and does nothing with it.

One caveat from the same literature, and it matters. <cite index="76-1">When there are few barriers to goal achievement, favourable goal intentions can suffice and implementation intention formation might be superfluous.</cite> So this should not fire every week. On a good week, the correct output is still nothing.

## Priya N., UX researcher

Two findings.

**On the home screen.** The current area card is a full card treatment carrying three short lines: an area label, an item title, a status line. That is list content wearing card clothing. <cite index="78-1">Cards win when each item carries rich, varied content. Lists win when the structure is shallow and mostly one line per item.</cite> Our content is shallow and uniform. Four identical cards each holding three lines is the wrong container.

The wider trend supports going tighter. <cite index="81-1">The products gaining ground in 2026 pack more information per screen while staying legible, and the difference between dense and cluttered is information hierarchy. Research on UI density shows minimalist interfaces can actually increase cognitive strain when users need to navigate multiple levels to find information.</cite>

The reframing I would offer, which is not "make everything smaller": <cite index="81-1">use hierarchy instead of removal. Before deleting an element, ask whether it could stay if it were smaller, lower contrast, or repositioned. Size, colour and position can deprioritise something without removing it.</cite>

**On the Report.** There is a hard number that should reshape it. <cite index="65-1">Research found over 70 percent of activity tracker usage is driven by glances, brief five second sessions where individuals check ongoing activity with no further interaction.</cite>

Our Report has no five second layer. It opens with a headline, then immediately requires reading. Most opens will be glances, and a glance at our Report currently returns the headline and nothing else. That is a wasted surface for the majority of sessions.

## Tomas L., editorial designer

The Report is my brief and my assessment is that it is currently a beautifully typeset wall of text. Karim is right.

The problem is uniform rhythm. Four sections, each with an all-caps gold label followed by prose at the same size, the same measure, the same weight, separated by identical spacing. That is not editorial design. That is a form.

<cite index="87-1">Rhythm sustains reading through consistent leading, measure and paragraph spacing. But variation has rules: pull quotes, sidebars and breakouts interrupt the pattern deliberately, not randomly.</cite> We have the rhythm and none of the interruption.

<cite index="91-1">Typography carries the hierarchy. Size contrast does the work that colour does elsewhere. Whitespace is intentional, not leftover: margins and gaps control pace the way silence controls music. Content breaks the grid on purpose. Pull quotes and inset elements should violate the column structure deliberately.</cite>

We have exactly one grid break, the headline, and then we never break it again.

<cite index="86-1">Good editorial work respects both skimmers and deep readers, because both behaviours exist on the same page.</cite> Ours respects only deep readers, and Priya's number says most sessions are skimmers.

One warning against overcorrection. <cite index="89-1">Editorial design is consistency over novelty, rhythm over surprise. A headline treatment that delights once becomes exhausting by the tenth spread.</cite> This report is read 52 times a year. Whatever variation we introduce must survive repetition. That rules out anything clever.

## Dr. Hanne V., clinical psychologist

I have two concerns and one endorsement.

**Endorsement.** The rolling fourteen day window with no streak is genuinely well designed and I want it protected in whatever follows. The decision to have no streak facts in the data model at all, so streak language cannot appear by accident, is the kind of structural safeguard most apps do not bother with.

**Concern one.** If Ines gets her implementation intentions, the obvious next step is measuring whether the user followed through. That step is where this app becomes something else. An app that notices you did not do the thing it suggested has introduced an authority relationship, and the entire language corpus is built on the premise that it has no authority. I will argue this in round two.

**Concern two.** The `selfReportVsData` family is the most powerful thing in the corpus and the most dangerous. *You called it deep work eleven days ago. It is still active.* Read on a good day that is insight. Read on a bad one it is the app catching you out. The line does no work the user could not do themselves; its only function is the small shock of being observed. That needs a rule, not just careful wording.

## Ray O., indie app operator

Three practical observations from having shipped this kind of thing.

**One.** The Report is the reason someone tells a friend about this app. Screenshot-ability is the entire distribution strategy for a solo developer with no marketing budget, and nobody screenshots a paragraph. Tomas's structural fix is not polish, it is the growth mechanism.

**Two.** Marcus is right about the fonts and I want to name the cost honestly. Changing the typeface changes the app's face. Karim has a shipped iOS app with this identity. He may reasonably decide that continuity with his own product matters more than avoiding a pattern most users have never consciously noticed. That is a legitimate call and the panel should present it as a choice rather than a verdict.

**Three.** Scope. We are proposing additions to a build that is already the largest in the portfolio, most of which needs no new engine work. I will hold the line on that in round three.

# ROUND TWO: DISAGREEMENT

## 2.1 Ines versus Hanne: does guidance need follow-through?

**Ines.** An implementation intention with no closure is a suggestion into the void. The literature on why intentions fail is precisely about the gap between forming a plan and acting. If the app never revisits it, we have added a better-worded suggestion and nothing structural.

**Hanne.** Revisiting is where you cross the line. Right now the app reports on what the user did. The moment it reports on what it told the user to do, it has appointed itself the party whose expectations are being met or missed. Every no-shame safeguard in the corpus becomes decorative.

**Ines.** Then make the check non-evaluative. Report the fact, not the compliance. Not "you did not do the thing," but the underlying number moving or not moving.

**Hanne.** That I can accept, with a condition. It must be indistinguishable from an ordinary observation. If a user could tell the difference between the app noticing a fact and the app checking on them, it fails.

**Priya.** There is a design answer here. Make the plan the user's rather than the app's. If the closing line is something the user can accept with a tap, it becomes their commitment, and reporting on your own commitment is not surveillance.

**Hanne.** That works, and it is the difference between a coach and a notebook. But it must be genuinely optional with no default and no nudge to accept.

**Ines.** Accepted. One tap, no consequence for ignoring it, and the follow-up is phrased as a fact about the data.

**Resolved.** Recommendation 12 and 13.

## 2.2 Marcus versus Ray: how far does the anti-slop remediation go?

**Marcus.** All six tells. If you fix five and keep the sixth you have a slightly better fingerprint.

**Ray.** The left border is free to fix, nobody will miss it. The typeface is a brand decision with real cost. Those are not the same class of change and presenting them as one list is how you get the whole list rejected.

**Tomas.** I will side partly with Marcus on a point neither of you raised. Instrument Serif is a *display* face. <cite index="60-1">It is described by its own foundry as a condensed display font intended for use at large sizes.</cite> We are using it at 26sp for Momentum headlines and Pulse observations, which are body-length sentences. It was never designed for that. The Report headline at 40sp is legitimate; the rest is a misuse independent of any slop question.

**Marcus.** That reframes it usefully. The problem is not that the serif is wrong, it is that it is doing two jobs and only qualified for one.

**Ray.** Then the proposal I can support is: keep a display serif for the moments that are genuinely display, change the sans, and introduce a proper text serif for reading-length serif content. That is defensible on craft grounds regardless of what any tell list says, which means it survives Karim disagreeing with the premise.

**Resolved.** Recommendations 2, 3 and 4, staged by cost.

## 2.3 Priya versus Marcus: density on the home screen

**Priya.** Slimmer rows, more visible at once, hierarchy doing the work.

**Marcus.** Careful. <cite index="78-1">Shrinking type to fit more rows trades readability for count.</cite> The item title is the single most important string on the screen. It does not get smaller.

**Priya.** Agreed, and I would not shrink it. The savings are in chrome, not content. Card padding, the shadow stack, the gap between cards, and the status line, which is currently a full row for information most users read once.

**Tomas.** The card itself is the biggest waste. <cite index="46-1">The prescribed separation order is whitespace first, then a 3 to 5 percent background lightness shift, then soft elevation, then a border only if all three fail.</cite> We went straight to elevation plus a border. Whitespace plus the existing colour wash would separate these perfectly well, and it removes a nested container at the same time.

**Marcus.** That I like, because it solves the density problem and the cardocalypse problem with one change, and it makes the wash structural rather than decorative.

**Priya.** One risk. Without a card edge, the swipe affordance gets weaker. Whatever moves needs a swipe test.

**Resolved.** Recommendations 7, 8 and 9.

## 2.4 Tomas versus Ray: how much structure does the Report get?

**Tomas.** It needs at least three distinct treatments: a glance layer, a reading layer, and a closing moment. Currently it has one treatment repeated four times.

**Ray.** Every treatment is a component to build and maintain. Give me the minimum that solves it.

**Tomas.** Three devices, and I will defend each. A **data moment** that is not prose, placed once, because the eye needs a rest and the skimmer needs something. A **varied measure**, meaning one section that is deliberately narrower or wider than the others, which is the cheapest possible grid break. And a **closing treatment** distinct from the sections above it, because the last thing read should not look like the fifth thing read.

**Priya.** The data moment also solves my glance problem, if it sits high enough.

**Ray.** Three is buildable. I would push the data moment above the fold and make it the only non-text element in the whole report, so it cannot proliferate.

**Marcus.** Agreed, with a constraint. It must not be a chart. <cite index="46-1">Gradients slapped on a big number for impact</cite> is on the tell list, and a sparkline in a card is the dashboard reflex. It should be typographic or it should be a single restrained mark.

**Resolved.** Recommendations 14 through 18.

## 2.5 The unresolved one: `selfReportVsData`

**Hanne.** I want a rule limiting it.

**Ines.** It is the single most valuable output in the app. It is the only place where the user's subjective account meets the record, which is exactly the sensemaking the literature says these tools fail to support.

**Hanne.** I am not asking to remove it. I am asking that it cannot fire in a report that is otherwise negative. Combined with a declining headline and a neglected area observation, it becomes a pile-on.

**Tomas.** That is a composition rule, and we already have a composition rules section. It belongs in the incompatibility matrix.

**Ines.** I accept that. I do not accept a frequency cap, because rarity is what gives it force.

**Hanne.** Agreed. Composition rule, no frequency cap, and a hard requirement that it never fires twice about the same subject.

**Resolved.** Recommendation 20.

# ROUND THREE: SYNTHESIS

The panel converged on a framing that reorganises everything above.

**Marcus.** The tells are not really about individual patterns. <cite index="47-1">The observation that matters is that the model reverts to the centre whenever a step is left open.</cite> Our design.md is highly specified, and it still produced six tells, because it was specified against a 2024 understanding. The durable fix is not a list of banned patterns. It is a rule that when the design system leaves a choice open, the answer is never the most common one available.

**Ines.** The parallel holds for the language. Our corpus is enormous and it is all observation. The engine has one job it never does: it never asks the user to decide anything except which of two words describes yesterday. Guidance is not more sentences. It is a different kind of turn.

**Hanne.** And the boundary that keeps that safe is authorship. The app observes. The user decides. Anything the app suggests must be acceptable or ignorable with no consequence either way.

**Tomas.** Structurally the same principle. The report currently tells you everything at one volume. Editorial pacing is deciding what the reader looks at first and what they may never read at all, and being at peace with the second category.

**Priya.** Which is the density argument too. <cite index="81-1">Hierarchy instead of removal.</cite> Nothing here needs deleting. Things need ranking.

**Ray.** So the through line is: rank things, and stop treating every element as equal. That applies to the card, the report, and the corpus. That I can build.

# RECOMMENDATIONS

Numbered for approval. Nothing has been implemented.

## A. AI slop remediation

**R1. Remove the coloured left border from the area card.** Replace the in-session indicator with a semantic one: the card's colour wash intensifies from 5 percent to roughly 9 percent, and the status line becomes a live countdown reading `In focus, 7 minutes left`. State is carried by the content, not by a decorative stripe.
*Cost: trivial. Confidence: very high. Unanimous.*

**R2. Replace Inter.** Move to a sans that is not the category default. Candidates, all open licensed and all bundleable: **Public Sans**, **Hanken Grotesk**, **Instrument Sans**, **Schibsted Grotesk**, **Figtree**. Marcus's preference is Hanken Grotesk for its slightly warmer humanist tone against the app's stance; Ray's is Public Sans for its neutrality and metric safety.
*Cost: low, one font swap plus a metrics pass. Confidence: high. Ray dissents on necessity, not on execution.*

**R3. Split the serif into two roles.** Keep a display serif for genuinely display moments only: the Report headline, onboarding statements, screen titles. Introduce a **text serif** for reading-length serif content: the Momentum headline, the Pulse observation, the Report body pull quotes. Candidates for the text serif: **Newsreader**, **Source Serif 4**, **Literata**, **Petrona**. This is defensible on craft grounds alone, independent of the slop question, since Instrument Serif is a condensed display face being asked to set sentences.
*Cost: moderate, two faces to license, subset and tune. Confidence: high.*

**R4. If R3 is accepted, reconsider Instrument Serif itself.** With the display role narrowed to four or five appearances, the case for keeping it weakens and the case for something with more character strengthens. Candidates: **Fraunces** at a low softness axis, **Bodoni Moda**, **Playfair Display** at an optical size, or retaining Instrument Serif on continuity grounds with the iOS app.
*Cost: brand decision. Confidence: medium. Panel split, presented as a choice.*

**R5. Retire all-caps section labels.** Replace `YOUR WEEK, HONESTLY` and every `sectionCaps` usage with one of: small caps in the text serif at reading size, or sentence case at reduced opacity preceded by a short rule. The all-caps gold label is on the tell list and it also fights the editorial voice.
*Cost: trivial. Confidence: high.*

**R6. Retire serif italic as an accent device.** The `ONE THING` line gets a different treatment, proposed in R17.
*Cost: trivial. Confidence: high.*

**R7. One separation device per element, never two.** Audit every component that currently carries both a hairline border and a shadow, and remove one. Chips, cards, settings rows, sheets. The prescribed order is whitespace, then a background lightness shift, then elevation, then a border only if all three have failed.
*Cost: low, a systematic pass. Confidence: high.*

## B. Home screen density

**R8. Dissolve the area card into a row.** Remove the white card container entirely. Separation comes from whitespace and the existing colour wash, which becomes structural rather than decorative. This removes a nested container, fixes the cardocalypse tell, and reclaims the padding and shadow budget in one change.
*Cost: moderate, and it requires a swipe affordance test. Confidence: high.*

**R9. Reduce chrome, not content.** The item title does not shrink; it is the most important string on the screen. Savings come from card padding, the shadow stack, inter-card gap, and folding the status line into a lighter treatment. Target: five areas comfortably visible where four currently are.
*Cost: low. Confidence: high.*

**R10. Rank the status line.** Currently every card spends a full row on `Active since Monday`, which most users read once. Drop it to caption weight at inkTertiary, or show it only for the idle and in-session states where it carries real information.
*Cost: trivial. Confidence: high.*

**R11. Re-test the swipe affordance after R8.** Without a card edge, swipe discoverability may degrade. If it does, the answer is a first-run hint rather than restoring the card.
*Cost: low. Confidence: medium, flagged as a risk rather than a certainty.*

## C. Guidance

**R12. Convert `ONE THING THIS WEEK` into an implementation intention.** Anchor the suggestion to a cue the app has already observed, in if-then form. Not *try finishing one thing already in a queue*, but *On Tuesday morning, before you add anything new, finish Book the venue deposit.* The evidence base is d equals 0.65 across 94 studies, with the mechanism being the specification of when, where and how rather than what.
*Cost: moderate. Requires a new fact category for cue detection (strongest day, most productive part of day, focus session timing) and a substantial rewrite of the 88 line ONE THING corpus. Confidence: very high on the evidence, high on the fit.*

**R13. Make the plan the user's, not the app's.** The closing line carries one optional tap that accepts it. Accepting stores it. Ignoring it costs nothing and is never mentioned. This converts a suggestion from an authority into a commitment the user authored, which is the difference between a coach and a notebook.
*Cost: moderate. Confidence: high. This is the concession that made Hanne accept R12.*

**R14. Follow through non-evaluatively, and only on accepted plans.** If a plan was accepted, next week's report may reference the underlying fact moving or not moving. It reports the number, never the compliance. It must be indistinguishable from an ordinary observation. If a user could tell the difference between the app noticing a fact and the app checking on them, the implementation has failed.
*Cost: moderate. Confidence: medium-high. This is the single riskiest recommendation in the set and the one I would most want you to interrogate.*

**R15. Guidance stays silent on good weeks.** The literature is explicit that when barriers are low, plan formation is superfluous. The existing `trustThePace` and `letItBe` families already do this; R12 must not erode them. The simulator floor of 12 percent stays and I would raise it to 15.
*Cost: none. Confidence: very high.*

## D. Report structure

**R16. Add a glance layer above the fold.** Over 70 percent of tracking app sessions are five second glances. The report currently returns only the headline in that window. Add one **data moment**: a single typographic element, high in the layout, that carries the week in one look. Not a chart, not a card, not a gradient number. A restrained typographic statement of the two or three numbers that define the week.
*Cost: moderate. Confidence: high.*

**R17. Give the closing line its own treatment.** The last thing read should not look like the fifth thing read. Distinct measure, distinct scale, generous space above it, and no italic. This replaces R6's removal with something better.
*Cost: low. Confidence: high.*

**R18. Break the grid exactly once, deliberately.** One section gets a different measure from the others, most likely the `PATTERN` section, since it is the one that reaches across weeks and deserves to feel different. Editorial pacing is interruption with rules, not variation for its own sake.
*Cost: low. Confidence: medium-high.*

**R19. Cap the total treatments at four.** Glance layer, section prose, the grid break, closing. This report is read 52 times a year and anything clever becomes exhausting by the tenth reading. Consistency over novelty.
*Cost: none, it is a constraint. Confidence: very high.*

**R20. Add `selfReportVsData` to the composition incompatibility matrix.** It may not fire in a report that already carries a declining headline or a neglected area observation. It may never fire twice about the same subject. No frequency cap, because rarity is what gives it force.
*Cost: trivial. Confidence: high.*

## E. The durable fix

**R21. Add an open-choice rule to design.md.** When the design system leaves a choice open, the answer is never the most statistically common option available. Specification alone did not protect us, because the specification was written before these patterns crystallised. This rule is what makes the system self-correcting as the tells shift.
*Cost: none. Confidence: high. This is Marcus's contribution and the panel considered it the most important single item.*

**R22. Add an anti-slop check to the verification checklist.** A named, dated list of current tells, checked before each release, with the expectation that the list changes over time. Treat it like a dependency that needs updating rather than a rule that stays true.
*Cost: low. Confidence: high.*

# Panel dissents, recorded

**Ray O. dissents from R2 and R4.** Not on the analysis, on the priority. His position: the typeface is the app's face, Karim has a shipped product with this identity, and continuity with his own work may reasonably outweigh a pattern most users never consciously name. He notes that two of six focus group members did name it, which he concedes weakens his position.

**Dr. Hanne V. registers a reservation on R14.** She accepts it as scoped but wants it built last, tested against the worst week the simulator can generate, and removed without argument if it reads as supervision.

**Ines R. notes that R12 without R14 is weaker than the evidence supports.** She accepts the trade as the price of R13.
